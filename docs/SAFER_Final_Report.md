# SAFER A Pre-Crash Severity Classification Research Prototype

**Tushar Pandey and Arpit Goel**  
Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Shiv Nadar University, Delhi-NCR, India

## Abstract

Road-collision severity modelling is difficult when source datasets have incomplete fields, duplicated records, inconsistent coverage, and rare Fatal outcomes. This paper documents SAFER, a reproducible research prototype for classifying Slight, Serious, or Fatal severity conditional on a hypothetical collision. The project began with an Indian highway accident dataset as a data-quality case study and then examined UK STATS19 collision records. Those experiments highlighted different constraints: limited usable information in the Indian sample and low Fatal-class precision in a larger but imbalanced UK dataset. Neither earlier experiment establishes pre-crash performance; the STATS19 feature set included number of vehicles, which is generally known only after impact. The revised SAFER model therefore excludes post-collision vehicle count. Its synthetic scenario generator includes a clearly marked hypothetical pre-impact speed and derives speed excess and a speeding indicator from it. A class-weighted Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and LightGBM are combined through entropy and disagreement weighting, with calibration and a Fatal alert threshold selected on development data. On the untouched synthetic Dataset A test set, SAFER obtained 84.75% accuracy, 84.91% balanced accuracy, and 84.98% macro-F1. A severe-imbalance Dataset C stress sample reached 85.65% accuracy but only 37.93% Fatal precision. All labels and speeds are generated by declared design assumptions, so these results measure recovery of a simulator rule and do not validate prediction on real roads.

**Index Terms—** road-collision severity, pre-crash features, adaptive ensemble, calibration, explainability, synthetic data.

## I. Introduction

Severity classification asks how serious a recorded or hypothetical collision is. It is distinct from predicting whether a collision will happen or forecasting the number of collisions. SAFER addresses the former task: severity conditional on a collision scenario. A model score is therefore not a crash-occurrence probability.

The project first examined an Indian highway accident dataset because of its local relevance. The documented audit found repeated rows, missing information, and limited coverage of some potentially useful predictors. The project then examined UK Department for Transport STATS19 collision data. That dataset offered more observations but a Fatal share of approximately 1.49% and the tested model had low Fatal precision. The STATS19 experiment also included number of vehicles involved, which is typically known only after a collision. It is a collision-severity experiment, not evidence for pre-crash prediction.

SAFER uses synthetic data to make its modelling process reproducible and to test class weighting, adaptive voting, calibration, explanation, and threshold behavior in a controlled setting. The synthetic experiment is not offered as a substitute for real-world validation.

### A. Contributions and research position

The contribution is an integrated evaluation workflow: instance-specific entropy and disagreement weighting, development-only calibration and Fatal threshold selection, local SHAP and global permutation explanations, and a severe class-prior stress sample. These components are established methods. SAFER does not claim to introduce a new base classifier or to demonstrate a consistent improvement over ordinary soft voting. The adaptive method is compared directly with uniform voting using paired bootstrap intervals; in this experiment, the intervals do not support a clear improvement.

## II. Related Work

Random Forests provide an established tree-ensemble approach [1], while gradient boosting and LightGBM provide complementary methods for structured tabular prediction [2], [3]. SHAP provides a framework for attributing individual model outputs to input features [4]. SAFER uses these established learners and explanation methods in a single reproducible experimental pipeline. Its novelty is limited to the combination and evaluation protocol; individual components are not presented as new.

The UK Department for Transport documents the coverage and coding of police-reported personal-injury collisions [5]–[7]. Such records describe reported collision outcomes; they do not by themselves measure exposure across all journeys or establish that all columns were available before impact.

## III. Methodology

### A. Dataset investigation

The initial Indian highway case study used the dataset described by Khanum et al. [8], [9]. The project audit reported 8,116 rows, 13 fields, and 4,063 repeated-row occurrences; retaining one copy of each exact duplicate left 4,053 unique rows. This audit provides context for the project history and is not the source used to train the revised synthetic model.

The STATS19 2024 collision extract documented in the earlier project contained 100,927 observations: 75,858 Slight (75.16%), 23,567 Serious (23.35%), and 1,502 Fatal (1.49%). Its chronological model experiment was a real collision-severity classification study, but it used number of vehicles as a feature. That field fails the revised pre-crash availability audit. Its metrics are therefore not presented as evidence for a pre-crash warning system and cannot be compared directly with the synthetic results below.

### B. Pre-crash feature availability

The revised schema excludes number of vehicles involved. Posted speed limit and actual vehicle speed are kept distinct. Since no measured or defensibly estimated vehicle-speed field is supplied for this synthetic project, `actual_speed_kmh_simulated` is explicitly hypothetical. The generator also derives `speed_excess_kmh_simulated = max(0, actual_speed_kmh_simulated - speed_limit_kmh)` and a binary speeding indicator. These fields are not telematics, police measurements, weather API results, or observations of a real crash.

| Feature group | Input status in SAFER |
|---|---|
| Date, day type, hour | Date is retained as metadata and excluded from prediction; day and hour are scenario inputs. |
| Posted speed limit | Legal road limit; it is not interpreted as actual speed. |
| Actual speed, speed excess, speeding indicator | Hypothetical simulated speed plus two derived fields; not observed data. |
| Road and junction attributes | Synthetic road type, junction configuration/control, and urban/rural context. |
| Environment | Synthetic weather, lighting, and surface fields. |
| Vehicle type and driver age band | Scenario fields assumed available for the synthetic demonstration. |
| Number of vehicles involved | Excluded because it is generally post-collision information. |
| Simulator noise, simulator risk score, severity | Label-generation audit/target fields; excluded from model predictors. |

The dataset contains 15 predictor columns. The scenario form asks for 13 values; speed excess and the speeding indicator are calculated from the two speed values.

### C. Synthetic generator and coefficient rationale

The category marginals are frozen from the earlier synthetic Dataset A and stored in `data/synthetic_context_marginals.json`. The generator samples each context field independently from its marginal distribution, except that day type is derived from date and hypothetical speed is derived from posted limit plus a declared random offset. The marginals are not real-world frequencies, and independent sampling can create combinations that require plausibility review.

Each input is mapped to a normalized design contribution in [0,1]. The weighted base score is

`R_base = .18r_limit + .16r_vehicle + .08r_road + .08r_junction + .04r_control + .04r_area + .08r_weather + .08r_light + .08r_surface + .06r_age + .04r_hour + .08r_speed_excess`.

Three designed interactions are

`I = .10(r_speed_excess × r_vehicle) + .08(r_weather × r_surface) + .06(r_light × r_hour)`.

The noisy score is `R = clip(R_base + I + ε, 0, 1.35)`, with `ε ~ Normal(0, 0.018²)`. Slight is assigned when R < 0.299, Serious for 0.299 ≤ R < 0.393, and Fatal when R ≥ 0.393.

| Parameter | Value | Qualitative design reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Posted speed limit | 0.18 | Higher relative weighting for road-speed context; legal limit only. |
| Vehicle category | 0.16 | Represents designed differences among categories. |
| Road type and junction detail | 0.08 each | Represents layout and junction context. |
| Junction control and area | 0.04 each | Smaller context contributions by design. |
| Weather, lighting, surface | 0.08 each | Represents environmental and visibility conditions. |
| Driver age band / hour | 0.06 / 0.04 | Adds demographic/time context to the simulator. |
| Speed excess | 0.08 | Replaces a post-collision count with a simulated pre-impact input. |
| Speed × vehicle interaction | 0.10 | Designed compounding term. |
| Weather × surface interaction | 0.08 | Designed compounding term. |
| Lighting × hour interaction | 0.06 | Designed compounding term. |
| Gaussian noise SD | 0.018 | Controlled variation; not estimated measurement error. |

The coefficients are qualitative simulator design parameters. They were not estimated using real crash observations, expert elicitation, or a causal study. They are not physical or official road-safety constants.

### D. Dataset splits and training

Dataset A contains 10,000 generated rows and is split with stratification into 60% fit, 10% calibration, 10% validation, and 20% untouched final test. Dataset B has 5,000 independent draws from the same simulator and is used only for evaluation. Dataset C is a 2,000-row no-replacement sample from B with 1,503 Slight, 470 Serious, and 27 Fatal rows. Its Fatal share is 1.35%, the nearest lower integer approximation to 1.37% in 2,000 rows. Because C is a subset of B, those evaluations overlap and are not independent replications.

The base learners are class-weighted Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and LightGBM. Their class probabilities are combined with uniform soft voting, entropy-adaptive weighting, and entropy plus disagreement penalties. Beta is selected from {0, 0.5, 1, 2} using validation Macro-F1. Platt and isotonic one-vs-rest calibrators are fit on the calibration partition; the method and Fatal threshold are chosen on validation data. The final run selected beta=2, isotonic calibration, and a Fatal alert cutoff of 0.40. The test sets are not used to choose these values.

### E. Explanations and sensitivity analysis

For individual scenarios, the app reports local permutation SHAP contributions for each model score. Global permutation importance is computed on the untouched Dataset A test set over five repeats. A separate label-rule sensitivity analysis varies selected simulator coefficients and thresholds while holding the fitted model fixed. It measures agreement with alternative synthetic labels; it is not a retrained-model result. The interactive scenario comparison displays model-score differences for two hypothetical inputs and is not a causal estimate.

## IV. Results and Discussion

### A. Main classification results

| Evaluation | Accuracy | Balanced accuracy | Macro-F1 | Fatal precision | Fatal recall |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Dataset A untouched test | 84.75% | 84.91% | 84.98% | 85.68% | 85.87% |
| Dataset B same-simulator sample | 84.68% | 84.80% | 84.89% | 85.42% | 85.42% |
| Dataset C, 1.35% Fatal | 85.65% | 84.01% | 72.18% | 37.93% | 81.48% |

On Dataset C, accuracy is high while Macro-F1 and Fatal precision are lower because the test contains only 27 Fatal cases. The validation-selected Fatal cutoff is a screening-style decision rule and creates a precision/recall trade-off. The alert should be shown separately from the three calibrated class scores.

### B. Ensemble comparison

On Dataset A, uniform soft voting achieved 84.50% accuracy and 84.67% Macro-F1. The selected beta=2 configuration achieved 84.50% and 84.61%. Paired bootstrap 95% intervals for beta=2 minus uniform voting were [-0.35, +0.35] percentage points for accuracy, [-0.39, +0.36] for balanced accuracy, and [-0.43, +0.31] for Macro-F1. All include zero, so the test does not establish a reliable adaptive-ensemble improvement.

### C. Calibration

| Dataset A test method | Multiclass Brier | Log loss | Macro OVR ECE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Raw | 0.2199 | 0.3699 | 0.0498 |
| Platt | 0.2127 | 0.3576 | 0.0333 |
| Isotonic, selected on validation | 0.2129 | 0.3471 | 0.0202 |

Calibration results are metric- and sample-dependent. On Dataset C, isotonic has Brier 0.2026 and log loss 0.3230, compared with raw Brier 0.1982 and log loss 0.3327. This does not establish calibrated probabilities for real collisions.

### D. Feature importance and simulator sensitivity

The largest mean Macro-F1 decreases after input permutation were vehicle type (0.2648), posted speed limit (0.1864), lighting (0.0935), simulated speed excess (0.0885), and weather (0.0762). These are model associations on synthetic test data, not causes.

Changing the Fatal label threshold from 0.393 to 0.413 changed 6.15% of held-out labels; the frozen model agreed with 83.40% of those alternative labels and Fatal recall was 94.77%. Lowering the cutoff to 0.373 changed 7.05%, agreement accuracy was 81.80%, and Fatal recall was 72.70%. The complete coefficient and threshold perturbation table is included in `outputs/simulator_sensitivity.csv`.

## V. Conclusion and Future Work

The revised SAFER package uses a feature-availability audit to remove number of vehicles from its synthetic pre-crash model and distinguishes posted speed limit from hypothetical simulated vehicle speed. It makes the label generator, coefficients, class thresholds, class imbalance stress test, calibration choices, comparison intervals, local and global explanations, and sensitivity analysis inspectable in the app and code. The integrated workflow is the project's defensible contribution; the experiment does not show a clear improvement over uniform voting and does not validate real-world prediction.

Before real-world claims, future work needs a representative and legally permitted dataset with predictors available before impact, measured or defensibly estimated actual speed, leakage and feature-timing review, temporal and geographic external testing, calibration under the intended deployment prior, and analysis of false-positive and false-negative costs. Until then SAFER should be described as a synthetic research prototype.

## Declaration and Data Availability

The Indian highway dataset is described in the public Zenodo record [8] and associated MethodsX article [9]. The UK STATS19 data source and documentation are listed in [5]–[7]. The final SAFER package includes synthetic Datasets A, B, C, the frozen marginal and risk-map specifications, generation and training code, fitted artifact, comparison and calibration outputs, and Streamlit interface. All generated records, speeds, and labels are synthetic. The project is not intended for operational or safety-critical use.
